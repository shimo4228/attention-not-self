# Three Models of Memory: Contemporary Science, Vasubandhu, Buddhaghosa

Notes on Ganeri, *Attention, Not Self*, ch. 12, "Autonoetic Consciousness and Ownership"
(Japanese version: [記憶の三つのモデル - Ganeri第12章（日本語）.md](<記憶の三つのモデル - Ganeri第12章（日本語）.md>))

---

## 0. Summary

- The fault line in this passage is not "science vs. Buddhism". It runs between **thin single-process models and thick dual-system models**, and it cuts across traditions. In Ganeri's line-up, Tulving sides with Buddhaghosa and Klein with Vasubandhu.
- **Vasubandhu ≈ Klein**: memory is the joint product of two systems, storage (ālaya-vijñāna) and ownership-tagging (kliṣṭa-manas). This is the most fine-grained mechanistic account, and it fits R.B. and Patient B. neatly. But ownership is a fabrication (vikalpa), so mental time travel itself, both episodic memory and future-oriented thought, falls on the side of delusion.
- **Buddhaghosa (Ganeri's reconstruction)**: episodic memory is an autonoetic *way of attending* to the past. It involves reliving, but no self-representation, no ownership, and no additional system.
- Ganeri's verdict is that Vasubandhu's storage is "too thin", since it sustains only a procedural sense of self. Manas is "too thick", since it makes a demand that autonoesis does not need to meet.

## 2. Strengths and weaknesses

### Contemporary science (Tulving / Klein)

- **Strengths**: there is empirical support from dissociations. R.B. lost ownership while keeping content. Patient B. lost episodic memory while keeping trait self-knowledge. R.B.'s recovery, "one by one, then all", suggests that ownership is a modular function.
- **Weaknesses**: Tulving admits the emergence of autonoesis is "mysterious". Klein's I-tag may be a label for the explanandum rather than an explanation of it.

### Vasubandhu

- **Strengths**:
  1. Positing storage (ālaya) and appropriation (manas) as *distinct consciousnesses* anticipates Klein's dual-system model by roughly 1,600 years.
  2. On the reading where vāsanā preserves *perspectival* information, it explains the context-dependence of episodic memory.
  3. It bridges gaps such as deep sleep, when perceptual consciousness lapses.
  4. Its two-layer account of the self predicts Patient B.
  
  This mechanistic resolution is what makes Yogācāra "very precise" on memory.
- **Weaknesses**: ownership is a fabrication, yet mental time travel requires ownership, so *episodic memory itself becomes delusion*. That is soteriologically consistent. As cognitive theory, though, it cannot distinguish normal remembering from delusion. The ālaya also carries the same continuity-bearer problem as bhavaṅga.

### Buddhaghosa (Ganeri's reconstruction)

- **Strengths**: it separates reliving from ownership and explains only the former, through a manner of attending. It posits no extra mechanism and no self-representation, so its ontological cost is minimal. It is soteriologically healthy, because remembering is not convicted of delusion. It fits the ATB (attentive true belief) thesis, which locates epistemic standing in the quality of attentive access rather than in propositional justification (here, a representation of ownership).
- **Weaknesses**:
  1. "Proprietary phenomenology" risks naming the explanandum rather than explaining it.
  2. The storage question is left open: what carries past content into the present?
  3. R.B.'s status wavers (next section).

## 3. Points for discussion

**a. The axis crosses traditions.** Tulving stands with Buddhaghosa, and Klein with Vasubandhu. Ganeri's argument is therefore not that "Buddhism anticipated science". It shows that *the same disagreement arises independently inside both traditions*.

**b. Vasubandhu's dilemma.** Holding both "ownership is fabricated" and "mental time travel requires ownership" makes memory delusive, and one premise has to go to avoid that result. Buddhaghosa drops the second. Within Yogācāra, the question becomes how ownerless remembering is possible after āśraya-parāvṛtti (轉識得智), when manas turns into samatā-jñāna.

**c. Ganeri's own tension (translator's note 8).** In the field/observer paragraph, R.B.'s episodic capacity is "intact" and serves as *confirmation* of Buddhaghosa's model. In the final paragraph, R.B.'s memory lacks proprietary phenomenology and is "not episodic", so R.B. becomes a *case of deficit*. Which reading one adopts changes how much empirical support Buddhaghosa's model has.

**d. R.B. seen from Vasubandhu.** If manas's appropriation is pure delusion, is R.B., having lost it, a step closer to liberation? Ganeri's phrase "not deluded but mind-blind" is an implicit answer: the absence of ownership is a deficit, not insight into non-self. R.B. still has a present self-sense, though, so manas has not ceased as a whole.
  - *The author's point (2026-10-28)*: the āśraya-parāvṛtti of manas is not the mere loss of a self-sense. As samatā-jñāna, it sees self and other as equal without losing the self-sense. So R.B. is not awakened.
  - *Supporting points*: (1) R.B. still owns "the fact that I had a degree from MIT", so his trait and semantic self-image is still appropriated as self. Manas is operating; only its link to episodic content is cut. (2) His past scenes feel "described by someone else", which means his past self has been *moved to the side of the other*. That redraws the self/other boundary instead of equalising it. (3) Yogācāra already distinguishes absences of mental function that are not liberation: in asaṃjñi-samāpatti (無想定) the six consciousnesses cease but manas continues, and it counts as a non-Buddhist false nirvāṇa. R.B. is a pathological counterpart. (4) His lack of grief at no longer walking looks like an absence of ātmasneha. But he does not suffer because he does not own the loss, whereas samatā-jñāna does not suffer while seeing through ownership. The two have different structures.

**e. Link to the bhavaṅga thesis.** Buddhaghosa's side rejects the ālaya, so what carries autobiographical content into the present? If Ganeri leans on bhavaṅga here, the continuity-bearer problem returns by another route, exactly as the author's critique predicts. If samanantara-pratyaya is read as a recurrence relation, storage can be treated as a *dispositional property of the causal series rather than a container*. That could complete Buddhaghosa's thin model without positing either ālaya or bhavaṅga, and this chapter is a good place to argue it.

**f. Preview.** Ganeri signals that Dignāga explains the same first-personal features through *reflexivity* (svasaṃvedana). The next section should therefore add a fourth model of self-knowledge, the reflexive one.
